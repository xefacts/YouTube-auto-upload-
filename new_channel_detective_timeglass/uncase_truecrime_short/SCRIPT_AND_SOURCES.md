# Short documentary — The Mona Lisa theft (1911)

## Exact narration used (153 words; 67.3-second final with breathing room)

August 21, 1911. The Louvre shut. By morning, the Mona Lisa was gone—and no one knew when.

The blank wall raised no alarm: paintings came down for photos and cleaning. So the absence looked routine.

Then the clue: Peruggia had worked inside, fitting protective glass. Investigators questioned him, but found no link. The trail went cold.

Two years later, he wrote Florence dealer Alfredo Geri, offering the painting. Geri called Uffizi director Giovanni Poggi. They checked it—and alerted police.

Your turn: if this was patriotism, why offer it for sale? Peruggia said he wanted to return it to Italy. The offer left motive in doubt.

In 1914, he was convicted and sentenced to one year and fifteen days. The Mona Lisa returned to the Louvre.

Final twist: the theft helped make a famous painting a global icon. Motive? Peruggia claimed patriotism; the sale suggested another possibility. What do you think—belief, profit, or both?

## Facts / careful wording

- Louvre Museum was closed on Monday, August 21, 1911; the theft was discovered the next day. The exact elapsed hours vary in secondary summaries, so narration says “by morning” rather than a disputed precise number.
- Louvre's own public educational video/transcript recounts the theft and its investigation; Library of Congress timeline places theft in 1911, recovery in Florence in December 1913, and the June 5, 1914 Florence sentence at one year and fifteen days.
- The ordinary-maintenance explanation for the blank wall is supported in Smithsonian reporting and the Louvre's public reconstruction; it explains delayed discovery, not intentional neglect.
- The Florentine State Archive preserves and describes the actual trial file and Peruggia case. It says he contacted dealer Alfredo Geri in November 1913; Geri consulted Uffizi director Giovanni Poggi.
- Peruggia's patriotic explanation is attributed to him, not presented as proven motive. His offer to sell makes motive ambiguous; the film does not declare a hidden accomplice or invent confession details.
- The Library of Congress notes the theft raised the painting's fame. Phrase “helped turn ... into a global icon” is a supported summary, not the claim that it had no fame beforehand.
- Sentence is the court's original term. Secondary accounts conflict on exact time served/reduction; we intentionally omit a time-served number.
- Louvre's public reconstruction includes accomplices; other accounts emphasize Peruggia. This short does not claim he physically acted alone or invent accomplices.
